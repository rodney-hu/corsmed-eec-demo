### The Mistake: Grading the Final Picture Instead of the Choices Behind It

A clean image can come from good planning or from a lucky guess, and the picture alone can't tell you which. Most scan assignments get graded on what comes out. The student submits an image, you look at contrast, noise and coverage, and you assign a score. The choices behind the image stay hidden, so you grade the result and miss the reasoning.

This mistake leads to the following:

- **Lucky images earn full marks:** A student who changed settings at random and landed on a good picture gets the same grade as one who planned it.

- **Planning errors hide in good images:** Weak slice coverage or off-center angulation can still produce an image that looks fine at a glance.

- **Students can't learn from a score:** "8 out of 10" doesn't say which choice cost the two points.

- **You learn at the externship site:** The site's first report tells you what your grade missed.

### The Reason This Happens

The picture is the easiest thing to look at. It sits on the screen, it's quick to compare, and it feels like the answer. The settings take more effort to find and more time to read. So the grade follows the picture.

Here are some more specific reasons:

- **Review takes time:** Reading a parameter list for 20 students is slower than glancing at 20 images.

- **Rubrics come from written exams:** One right answer per question carries over to scans.

- **The reference answer is an image:** Answer keys often include the picture and skip the settings.

- **Some tools hide the inputs:** Not every practice setup shows the settings next to the image.

### How To Fix It

Grade the choices, and let the image be the evidence. When you review the settings and the slice plan, you see the thinking behind the picture. Students then get feedback on the thing they control, and that's what they can change next time.

Instead of grading the picture alone, do this:

- **Collect the settings and slice plan with every image:** Ask for the parameter list and a planning screenshot alongside the picture.

- **Score planning separately from the result:** Give points for coverage, angulation and parameter choices, and give points for the image.

- **Ask for one sentence of why:** "I raised the averages because the image was grainy" takes 20 seconds to write and shows whether the student understood.

- **Run a reference scan yourself:** Compare each submission to it, so students see how close they came and where they drifted.

- **Track each student across weeks:** A simple log by student shows who improves and who stalls, before an externship report tells you.
