### The Mistake: Assigning "Go Practice On Your Own"

"Go practice on your own" is the assignment that never gets done. Nothing is due. Nothing is graded. Nobody can see who practiced and who didn't. A practice tool with no structure turns into an unused license.

This mistake leads to the following:

- **The login nobody opens:** You set up access. A few self-starters use it and the rest forget it exists.

- **Students practice the wrong thing:** Without a target, they open familiar scans and repeat what they already do well.

- **No feedback loop:** A student can repeat a poor habit 50 times with nobody there to say so.

- **You find out late:** The first sign that someone never practiced is a weak externship report.

### The Reason This Happens

Adult learners are supposed to direct their own study, and that belief does a lot of quiet damage here. It sounds respectful. It also removes every cue a student needs to start. So practice slips behind the next exam, then the next one.

Here are some more specific reasons:

- **Access feels like the assignment:** Giving students the tool feels like finishing the job.

- **The schedule has no room:** Lectures, labs and exams fill the week, and practice has no slot.

- **Usage is hard to see:** Without reports, "I practiced" and "I didn't" look the same.

- **Students can't tell what good looks like:** Without a target, they can't tell whether a session counted.

### How To Fix It

Give every session a target, a time box and a debrief. A target tells the student what to do. A time box makes it small enough to start. A debrief turns the session into learning because the student has to say what changed and why.

Instead of "go practice," do this:

- **Set one target per session:** "Produce one axial brain image with the least noise you can get, then write down the settings you used."

- **Time-box it:** 30 minutes of practice, then a 5-minute debrief. What did you change, what happened, why?

- **Put it on the schedule:** Group sessions with an educator in the room give students a slot and give you feedback in real time. A short intro is enough to start. Noyal Mathew, an MRI practice educator at UCLH, says on Corsmed's site: "A 1-hour introduction enables our learners to explore independently."

- **Check usage weekly:** Look at who practiced. Message the students who didn't in week two, not week ten.

- **Make progress visible:** Post a simple completion list. Students respond to a list with their name on it.
