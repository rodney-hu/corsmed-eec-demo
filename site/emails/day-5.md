### The Mistake: Teaching One Scanner's Buttons Instead of the Principles

Students learn the scanner you hand them. They memorize where things live on your console and follow your click path. Then they rotate to a site with a different vendor, and the screen looks like a foreign language. The skill never moved with them.

This mistake leads to the following:

- **Freezing at the next console:** A student who knows one menu layout hunts for the same setting under a different name.

- **Teaching turns into translation:** You spend class time on vendor labels instead of the idea.

- **Confidence drops on rotation:** A student with one vendor's habits feels like a beginner again at a second site.

- **Mixed-vendor sites expose the gap fast:** A site with scanners from more than one vendor can show it in a single shift.

### The Reason This Happens

You teach on the scanner you have, and that's a reasonable choice. The manual is vendor-specific. The lab is vendor-specific. Students ask "which button?" and you answer, because the answer is right there in front of you.

Here are some more specific reasons:

- **Your equipment sets the lesson:** The console in the lab becomes the curriculum.

- **Manuals and handouts are vendor-specific:** The easiest material to hand out is the vendor's own.

- **Students ask for buttons:** "Where do I click?" is a faster question to answer than "what are you trying to change?"

- **You can't predict the placement site:** Students may land on any vendor, so no single console prepares all of them.

### How To Fix It

Teach the shared workflow first and treat the console labels as the small part. Every console asks the student to do the same jobs: set up the exam, plan the slices, choose a sequence, adjust parameters and check the image. When students learn those jobs, a new menu is a new set of labels and the thinking carries over.

Instead of teaching buttons, do this:

- **Teach the workflow in vendor-neutral terms:** Walk through the jobs in order before you show any console.

- **Teach the goal of each setting before its name:** Ask "what are you trying to change?" first, then "where is it on this console?"

- **Give each vendor a one-page translation sheet:** Setting names side by side. Students carry it on rotation.

- **Test on a console they haven't seen:** Put students on an unfamiliar interface once before placement and watch what they do.

Noyal Mathew of UCLH describes the payoff on Corsmed's site: "I can focus on explaining how the physics work."
