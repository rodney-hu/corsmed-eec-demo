### The Mistake: Teaching Physics Equations First

A student can write out the definitions of TR and TE from memory and still stare at the console when the image comes back grainy. You taught the physics. They passed the quiz. The pieces never connected to a picture. That gap shows up the first time a student has to choose a setting instead of define one. Equations first and images second produces students who know the answer on paper and have no feel for the dial.

This mistake leads to the following:

- **The exam-pass, console-blank student:** They score well on the written test. At the console they can't predict what happens when they change one setting, so they change three and hope.

- **Parameters learned as a list:** TR, TE, flip angle, bandwidth and averages sit in memory as separate facts. Nobody has shown them how one change pulls on the others.

- **Trade-offs that stay invisible:** Signal, resolution and scan time compete with each other. An equation says that. A pair of images makes a student feel it.

- **Office hours that turn into re-teaching:** The same students come back a week later and ask you to explain TE again, because the first explanation never had a picture attached.

### The Reason This Happens

Equations feel rigorous, and rigor feels safe to teach. They fit on a slide. They have one right answer. They match the order of most textbooks. So the equation goes first, and the picture waits for lab time that may never come.

Here are some more specific reasons:

- **You learned it this way:** Most educators got the physics first and the pictures later. Teaching in the order you learned feels natural.

- **Equations are easy to grade:** A formula question has a clear answer. "Explain why this image is noisy" takes longer to mark.

- **Live demos are hard to run:** Changing a setting in front of a class needs scanner time or software. Most classrooms have neither on a Tuesday afternoon.

- **The textbook sets the order:** Chapters run from fundamentals to images, and the syllabus follows the chapters.

### How To Fix It

Flip the order. Show the image change first, then give the equation a job. When students see what a setting does, the formula becomes the explanation for something they've already watched happen. That's when it sticks.

Instead of opening with the equation, do this:

- **Show one parameter, two images:** Change only TE. Put both images on screen. Ask what changed before you say a word about the formula.

- **Make students predict first:** Before each change, have them write down what the image will do. Wrong predictions teach more than right ones.

- **Name the trade-off every time:** After each change, ask which of signal, resolution or scan time the setting just bought or spent.

- **Give the formula a job:** Once students have seen the effect, show the equation as the reason behind it.

- **Open class with a 10-minute demo:** One setting change per class, same routine every time. After a few weeks, students predict before you ask.
